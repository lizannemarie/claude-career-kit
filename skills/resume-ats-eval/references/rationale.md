# Why this skill is structured the way it is

Read this if you want the reasoning behind the workflow, not just the steps — e.g. explaining the approach to the user, or deciding how to handle an edge case the steps don't cover explicitly.

## The core premise

Most mid-size and large companies run resumes through some form of automated screening before a human ever reads them — Greenhouse's Talent Matching, Workday's Skills Cloud, Lever, iCIMS, Eightfold.ai, etc. Candidates rarely get to see how they scored, so this skill builds an honest, transparent proxy: derive a scoring rubric straight from the job posting's own language, score the resume against it with visible math, and use that to guide real, honest resume improvements — not keyword-stuffing or fabrication.

Every score should be explainable ("here's the weight, here's the match %, here's the math"), and every resume change should trace back to something true about the person, either already on their resume or confirmed by them directly. Never invent a credential, title, tool, or metric to raise a score.

## The score is a proxy, not a verdict

A tailored resume that clears the scoring threshold through honest reframing can still be a poor bet if what's left uncovered is a hard, structural requirement (a required degree, certification, title/level, or years in a specific named tool) rather than a framing issue. Real ATS platforms and human recruiters often filter on exactly those structured fields regardless of how well the surrounding narrative reads. This is why the skill treats hard-filter risk (step 6a) as a first-class output, not a footnote.

## Two distinct filters, not one

Between "application submitted" and "hiring manager reads this," there are usually two separate gates, and they respond to different things:

- **The machine stage** — ATS parsing and keyword/skills-graph ranking. A resume can be silently deprioritized here with no rejection ever recorded (a posting sitting unanswered for weeks is as informative as an explicit rejection).
- **The human recruiter-scan stage** — a 10-20 second skim, usually anchored on the headline/summary — that only happens once something clears the first gate.

Tailoring a resume per role/per "hat" the person has genuinely held is correct targeting for the machine stage and is never a problem on its own — a cold recruiter at Company A never sees the version tailored for Company B, so there is no cross-application "coherence" penalty to worry about there. The one place inconsistency could plausibly surface is a public, singular profile (e.g. LinkedIn) that a recruiter might check after the resume clears a screen — that's a LinkedIn-consistency question, not a reason to avoid per-role resume tailoring, and it only matters once the machine-stage gate is being cleared in the first place.

When helping someone figure out where their applications are actually getting stuck, help them tell these two gates apart rather than assuming a low contact rate is about resume framing quality or about how many different roles they've tailored toward.

## Language checklist (fallback when the Humanizer skill is not installed)

Scan every new or edited line for: em-dash overuse and repeated punctuation tics; reflexive buzzwords ("results-driven", "proven track record", "spearheaded", "leverage synergies"); hollow enthusiasm that asserts instead of demonstrating; uniform sentence rhythm; adjective piles in place of one concrete detail; not-X-but-Y contrasts; forced groups of three. Rewrite flagged lines as plain, specific statements grounded in a number, a named tool, or a named outcome. Keep lines the person already wrote well in their own voice.
