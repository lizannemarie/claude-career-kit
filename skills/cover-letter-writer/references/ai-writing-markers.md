# AI writing markers checklist

Read the letter straight through once, specifically looking for each of these. They're individually small, but a letter with several of them stacked up reads as machine-written even when the content is true and specific.

## 1. Em-dash overuse

The single most common tell. A cover letter doesn't need more than one, if that. For each em-dash, replace it with whichever is more natural:
- A period (splitting into two sentences)
- A comma
- A colon (if it's introducing an explanation or list)
- Parentheses (if it's a true aside)

Watch for hyphens or en-dashes being used the same way ("thing - like this") — same problem, same fix.

## 2. Repeated root words

Using the same word or root 3-4 times across a short letter is a distinctive LLM pattern — humans vary their language more without noticing; models fixate on whatever word appeared in the prompt or an earlier sentence. Common repeat offenders in cover letters: "adopt/adoption," "leverage," "drive/driving," "instinct," "throughline," "passion/passionate," "excited/excitement." Scan for any content word appearing more than twice and swap at least half the instances for a plain synonym or a restructured sentence.

## 3. Stock phrases and clichés

Flag and rewrite anything from this family:
- "I'm excited/thrilled to apply for..."
- "perfect fit," "unique blend of skills," "proven track record" (fine once, suspicious twice)
- "fast-paced environment," "wear many hats," "hit the ground running"
- "seamless," "robust," "cutting-edge," "synergy," "paradigm"
- "is a dream," "would be an honor," "dream come true"
- "not just X, but Y" construction, especially if used more than once
- Tricolon overload — three parallel adjectives/clauses in a row, repeated as a rhythm across multiple sentences ("strategic, technical, and collaborative... analytical, thorough, and scale-oriented...")

These aren't wrong in isolation; the tell is when a letter leans on the pattern itself rather than on specific content.

## 4. Hollow enthusiasm / exclamation stacking

More than one exclamation point in a professional letter reads as performed rather than genuine. Cut extras; let specific, concrete details carry the enthusiasm instead of punctuation.

## 5. Punctuation inconsistency

Pick "and" or "&" and use it throughout — don't let both appear in the same letter (a common tell of a draft assembled or edited in pieces). Same for serial commas, colons vs. semicolons for lists, etc. — consistency reads as human care; inconsistency reads as stitched-together text.

## 6. Grammar slips from reshuffling

When sentences get restructured (by a human or a model) to add clauses, verbs sometimes stop agreeing with the new subject ("the foundations of which enabled our teams stop re-solving" — missing "to"). Read each long, multi-clause sentence in isolation and check it parses as one clean sentence.

## 7. Generic where it should be specific

Not a punctuation issue, but the deeper version of all of the above: any sentence that would still be true if you deleted the company name and job title is doing too much rhetorical work and not enough factual work. Prefer a real tool name, a real number, a real team name, a real quote from a colleague over an abstract claim about skills or character.
